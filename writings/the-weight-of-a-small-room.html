The Weight of a Small Room

27 July 2026

The first thing you notice about a small room is not its size but its acoustics. Sound has nowhere to go. A cough returns to you slightly altered, and after a week you begin to recognise the alteration as the room’s own voice — a thing you are in conversation with whether or not you consent to the exchange.

This is an example post, published so you can see the whole workflow running: Markdown in posts/, a page and a PDF out the other side, an entry on the writings index, an item in the RSS feed. Delete it whenever you like, or copy it as the starting point for something real.

A section heading

Sections break the argument into places a reader can stand. Below the heading, the prose resumes at the same measure, so the eye never has to relearn where lines begin.

Markdown handles the usual inline things: a link, bold text, italics, and monospace for a filename mid-sentence. Quotes and dashes are curled automatically – this becomes an em dash, and “these” become proper quotation marks.

A quotation sits apart from the body, marked by a rule in the accent colour. It should hold several lines without losing the thread.

A smaller heading

Lists are for when the prose would otherwise become a sequence of clauses joined by semicolons:

  • An unordered item, stated plainly.
  • A second item, longer, to show how an entry wraps past a single line.
  • A third, for rhythm.

And when order matters:

  1. First, establish the problem.
  2. Then, complicate it.
  3. Finally, decline to resolve it.

Longer code gets its own block, which scrolls rather than forcing the page to:

<article class="post">
        <h1>Title</h1>
      </article>

The last paragraph should end without ceremony. The room is small; you simply stop talking and the sound stops with you.
