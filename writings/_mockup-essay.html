The Weight of a Small Room

27 July 2026

The first thing you notice about a small room is not its size but its acoustics. Sound has nowhere to go. A cough returns to you slightly altered, and after a week you begin to recognise the alteration as the room's own voice — a thing you are in conversation with whether or not you consent to the exchange.

This is the opening paragraph of a piece of writing that does not exist. Its only job is to show what a paragraph looks like here: the measure of the line, the space between lines, the colour of the text against its background. Read two or three of these and you will know whether the typography is comfortable enough to carry three thousand words.

A section heading

Sections break the argument into places a reader can stand. A heading should be short enough to scan and specific enough to promise something. Below it, the prose resumes at the same measure, so the eye never has to relearn where lines begin.

Inline elements need somewhere to live too. Here is a link to another page, some bold text for emphasis, some italics for a title or a foreign phrase, and a fragment of monospace for when a piece of code or a filename has to appear mid-sentence.

A quotation sits apart from the body, marked by a rule in the accent colour. It should be able to run several lines without losing the thread, and it should look deliberate rather than accidental.

A smaller heading

Third-level headings handle the subdivisions inside a section. They are set in the display face like everything else structural, but smaller, so the hierarchy stays legible at a glance rather than requiring the reader to measure.

Lists are for when the prose would otherwise become a sequence of clauses joined by semicolons:

  • An unordered item, stated plainly.
  • A second item, slightly longer, to show how a list entry wraps when it runs past the end of a single line.
  • A third, for rhythm.

And when order matters:

  1. First, establish the problem.
  2. Then, complicate it.
  3. Finally, decline to resolve it.

Longer code or configuration gets its own block, which scrolls horizontally rather than forcing the page to:

<article class="post">
  <h1>Title</h1>
  <time datetime="2026-07-27">27 July 2026</time>
</article>

A horizontal rule marks a turn — a break in argument too large for a paragraph but too small for a heading. After it, the piece can change its mind.

The last paragraph should end without ceremony. No summary, no restatement of the thesis, no farewell to the reader. The room is small; you simply stop talking and the sound stops with you.
