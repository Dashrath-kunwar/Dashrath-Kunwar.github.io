Parasnath

Somewhere in the Chota Nagpur plateau, rising out of the Giridih district of Jharkhand, a single hill holds more spiritual weight per square foot than almost anywhere else in the subcontinent. It’s called Parasnath by most maps, Sammed Shikharji by the people who actually climb it, and to a Jain it is not a hill at all. It’s the place where liberation happened, over and over, for twenty of the twenty-four Tirthankaras. Only two other sites in the world are held in comparable regard, and neither has this one’s density of it: twenty enlightenments, one hill, a single ridge of rock roughly 4,480 feet above the plain.

The mountain of endings#

In Jain cosmology, moksha, final liberation from the cycle of birth and death, is the entire point of the practice. Most traditions mark the place where a teacher was born, or taught, or died, as sacred by association. Shikharji marks something more specific: the literal geographic point where a soul stopped being reborn. Parshvanatha, the twenty-third Tirthankara and the figure the hill is popularly named for, is said to have attained nirvana here. So did nineteen others before him. That repetition is the whole argument for the mountain’s sanctity: not one saint choosing one spot, but two dozen, independently, across who knows how many centuries, ending up at the same ridge.

Whether you read that as history, as accreted legend, or as something in between, the effect on the ground is the same. The mountain functions as a single continuous shrine. Devotees don’t visit one temple at the base and call it done. They climb.

The climb#

The pilgrimage route runs roughly 27 kilometers round trip from the town of Madhuban at the base, up and over the ridge, past a scattered constellation of shrines called tonks, each one marking the specific spot where a particular Tirthankara is believed to have achieved liberation. There are more than twenty of them strung along the route, which means the climb isn’t a single ascent to a single summit so much as a slow circuit through two dozen separate endings.

Devout pilgrims do it barefoot. They start before dawn, in the dark and the cold, specifically so the climb and the descent both happen without the sun directly overhead, and so the whole circuit can be finished in a single day, since nothing is supposed to eat after sundown. Palanquins exist for those who can’t manage the distance on foot, carried by porters who make the same climb daily for a living, which is its own kind of endurance the pilgrimage doesn’t usually get credit for.

What strikes most first-time visitors isn’t any single temple. It’s the fact of the walking itself, hours of it, in silence, past shrine after shrine, until the specific one you started out for stops being the point.

A hill in dispute#

In late 2022 and into 2023, the Jharkhand government moved to designate parts of Parasnath as an eco-tourism zone, opening the area up to a wider kind of visitor than the pilgrimage traffic it had always drawn. Jain communities across India responded with alarm, then protest, then a wave of demonstrations large enough to make national news. The objection wasn’t to visitors generally. Sammed Shikharji had always had them. It was to the category shift: from tirtha, a place you approach with fasting and bare feet and silence, to tourist destination, a place you visit with none of that required.

The practical fears were specific: alcohol and meat entering a site where both are anathema, foot traffic and construction disturbing what pilgrims consider consecrated ground, the whole texture of the place changing under the weight of a different kind of attention. The protests eventually pushed the central government to intervene and walk the eco-tourism designation back. But the episode is worth remembering on its own, because it’s a clean example of a fight that a lot of old sacred places eventually have: not whether people should keep coming, but what kind of attention the place is allowed to ask of them.

Why it stays with me#

I haven’t made the climb. What holds my attention about Parasnath isn’t the specific theology, which I’ll admit I only half understand, but the shape of the devotion itself: a mountain treated not as a monument to one moment but as a kind of accumulated record, shrine after shrine, each one insisting that this exact spot mattered enough to end a cycle that had otherwise never ended. That’s a strange and moving thing to build a landscape out of. Twenty separate full stops, on one ridge, and people still walking barefoot between them to read each one in turn.
